5  Marovoay-Ankarafantsika

The Marovoay rural observatory offers an outstanding opportunities to study the socio-economic impacts of protected areas in Madagascar. This is because its panel data coincides both spatially and temporally with the expansion of Ankarafantsika, one of the country’s oldest and most emblematic protected areas. By comparing villages located on different sides of the Betsiboka river, we can build a quasi-experimental design to estimate how the 2002 reclassification of the reserve into a national park affected local households.

Warning

Note that this is very rough result at this stage. We need to refine the variable calculation, perform outlier identification, carefully reconsider the choice of outcome variables (on education and food security), strenghten model specification and conduct robustness checks.

5.1 Protected area expansion

5.1.1 Ankarafantsika before 2002

Ankarafantsika was first created as a Réserve Naturelle Intégrale (RNI) in 1927, with boundaries covering only a fraction of today’s surface:

Septième réserve naturelle. — Plateau d’Ankarafantsika, situé dans le district de Marovoay, région de Majunga, superficie approximative : 66.541 hectares, ayant pour limites: Au nord, la propriété dite: « Sainte-Marie Marovoay », titre n° 784-M,dts terrains domaniaux et, à une distance de cinq kilomètres environ, les villages d’Ampombomangarakaraka et Beronono ; A l’est, des terrains domaniaux; Au sud, des terrains dumaniaux et, à quelque distance, des villages d’Ankorika et de Bevasaka ; A l’ouest, des terrains domaniaux et la rivière Mahajamba. Tel au surplus que cette réserve naturelle est figurée à l’un des trois plans annexés audit arrêté. Décret du 31 décembre 1927

Over the decades, its legal status evolved through a series of decrees and classifications:

la Réserve Naturelle Intégrale d’Ankarafantsika fut créée en 1927 et ses limites ont été précisées en 1966 avec le Décret n° 66-242 du 1 juin 1966 (60 520 ha). Parallèlement, des territoires de la Forêt d’Ankarafantsika sur les districts de Marovoay et Ambato Boeny ont été classés réserves forestières avec l’Arrêté du 24 décembre 1929 (75 000 ha). De plus, le Jardin botanique d’Ampijoroa (district d’Ambato Boeny, 91 ha), recensé en 1969 par le Domaine Forestier National est devenu la Station Forestière d’Ampijoroa (4974 ha) en 1999 par l’Arrêté interministériel n° 13-056/99 du 7 décembre 1999. En 2002, la réserve naturelle intégrale créée en 1927 et reclassée en 1966, les réserves forestières instituées en 1929 ainsi que la Station Forestière d’Ampijoroa sont toutes instituées en un seul parc national (Décret n° 2002-798 du 7 août 2002, 130 000 ha). Ce dernier subit un changement de limites en 2015 sous le Décret n° 2015-730 du 21 avril 2015 ; Dernière modification de statut : suivant le Décret n° 2015-730 du 21 avril 2015 ; Surface actuelle : 136 513 ha (selon le décret en vigueur). (Goodman et al. 2018, vol. 2, p. 157)”

The extension of the protected area in 2002 was mapped as follows in 2002 by experts from the NGO Conservation International, which spearheaded the initiative.

The objective of this study is to assess the impact of the 2002 expansion on the populations of villages that became adjacent to the RNI at that time.

Some details on the status of the forest reserve that was transformed into an RNI during the expansion:

Par arrêté du Gouverneur Général p. i., en date du 24 décembre 1929, pris en conseil d’administration, est mise en réserve, dans les conditions fixées par les articles 50 à 52 du décret du 28 août 1913, la portion du massif forestier dit de l’Ankarafantsika, situé à l’ouest de la 7e réserve naturelle créée par le décret du 31 décembre 1927, dans la province de Marovoay ; La réserve ainsi instituée, d’une surface approximative de 75.000 hectares telle qu’elle est figurée au plan ci-annexé, est limitée: Au nord, par une ligne droite partant de l’angle N. W. de la 7e réserve naturelle et allant jusqu’au village d’Ampombilavakely, puis de ce village jusqu’à la limite de la propriété dite « Croix Rousse » titre n°3153; A l’ouest, par les propriété dites « Croix Rousse et Mont-rouge » titre no 3153 — M. et 2970 — M jusqu’au vilhge de Bongomaro ; Au sud, par la concession n°1059, jusqu’au nord du village de Beilalana, puis; par une ligne droite passant par le village de Bedo et allant jusqu’à l’angle S. W. de la 7e réserve naturelle ; A l’est, par la 7e réserve naturelle.

Articles 50 to 52 of the 1913 Forest Code refer to the forest reserve regime. However, there is little information on what this entails in terms of rules. More importantly, subsequent analysis reveal that there was no effective control over the forest reserve (Brondeau 1999).

This map shows the four relevant sites: Bepako and Madiromiongana, located on the same side of the Betsiboka river as Ankarafantsika, and Ampijoroa and Maroala, located across the river.

Although the latter two appear geographically close “as the crow flies,” in practice the Betsiboka forms a major natural barrier. Crossing is possible only under certain hydrological conditions, often requiring long detours or costly boat transport. In practice, flows of people, goods, and services are much denser along the Marovoay downstream axis than across the river. This physical separation strengthens the plausibility of using the cross-river sites as a control group.

5.3 Available observations

5.3.1 Marovoay observatory only

To proceed with the evaluation, we document the size of the household panels in each site and year, and assign treatment status. The treatment definition follows the 2002 expansion: households in Bepako and Madiromiongana are coded as “treated” (pre-2002 vs. post-2002), while Ampijoroa and Maroala serve as untreated controls.

# Function to rename variables using a named list
# e.g. renames = list("j0" = c("obsr", "obsr96"))
rename_all_custom <- function(df, renames) {
  if (length(renames) == 0) return(df)
  for (new_name in names(renames)) {
    # Skip renaming if new_name already exists in df
    if (new_name %in% names(df)) next
    old_names <- renames[[new_name]]
    matched_old <- old_names[old_names %in% names(df)]
    if (length(matched_old) > 0) {
      df <- df %>% rename(!!new_name := all_of(matched_old[1]))
    }
  }
  return(df)
}

# Generic function to load and standardize records from any year
# Arguments:
#   - year: numeric year of the survey
#   - vars: vector of variables to select (after renaming)
#   - renames: named list of renaming rules (new_name = c(possible_old_names))
load_records <- function(year, file_name, vars, renames = list()) {
  path <- paste0("data/ROS_MDG_microdata/", year, "/")
  df <- read_dta(paste0(path, file_name))

  df <- rename_all_custom(df, renames)

  # Ensure all expected vars exist (fill missing with NA)
  missing_vars <- setdiff(vars, names(df))
  df[missing_vars] <- NA

  # Handle j0 if not in original file
  if (!"j0" %in% names(df)) {
    df <- df %>%
      mutate(j0 = substr(j5, 1, 2)) %>% 
      mutate(j0 = as.character(as.numeric(j0)))
  }

  df %>%
    mutate(j0 = as.character(as.numeric(j0))) %>%
    select(any_of(vars))
}

The resulting heatmap presents three key insights:

  • Early years (1995–1997): only the two treated sites (same side of the river) were included in the survey. This means that the earliest baseline lacks control villages, which will lead to discard these years from comparisons.
  • Post-1998: the two control sites were added, creating a four-site structure that enables a difference-in-differences design.
  • Attrition and coverage: while household counts vary across years, the overall panel remains sufficiently populated to support longitudinal analysis.

5.3.2 Larger control pool

While the Marovoay observatory offers a natural treated–control comparison across the Betsiboka river, restricting the control group to its two sites limits statistical power. We therefore enlarge the control pool to include all other ROS sites that have both pre- and post-2002 observations. This approach relies on the same identification strategy — comparing households adjacent to the expanded Ankarafantsika park to those in unaffected areas — but increases sample size and improves the precision of estimates.

# Function to run gsynth for a given outcome
run_gsynth <- function(outcome, data) {
  formula <- as.formula(paste0(outcome, " ~ treatment + n_membres"))
  gsynth(
    formula,
    data = data,
    index = c("site_id", "year"),
    se = TRUE,
    inference = "parametric",
    r = c(0, 3),
    CV = TRUE,
    force = "two-way",
    parallel = TRUE,
    min.T0 = 4,
    nboots = 100,
    seed = 2139
  )
}

5.4 Results (provisional and subject to change)

Important

Note that this is very rough result at this stage. We need to refine the variable calculation, perform outlier identification, carefully reconsider the choice of outcome variables (on education and food security), strenghten model specification and conduct robustness checks.

The figures plot event-study coefficients relative to the year of expansion, while tables report average treatment effects on the treated (ATT).

The synthetic control estimations do not show systematic evidence of income gains for households living adjacent to Ankarafantsika following the 2002 PA extension. For total income (revtot) and constructed income (revcou), the average treatment effects are negative, though not statistically significant, with confidence intervals spanning both positive and negative values. The time profiles suggest some short-term increases around the mid-2000s, followed by a decline, but the uncertainty margins remain wide.

Disaggregating by income source points to a similar pattern. Agricultural revenues (rev_agri_current) display negative average effects, while non-agricultural revenues (rev_nonagri_current) show no consistent trend, with only a brief positive deviation in the mid-2000s. Taken together, these results suggest that households did not experience sustained income growth as a result of the park expansion, and if anything, agricultural activities may have been more adversely affected.

The composition of household activities also does not show discernible shifts. Neither the share of agriculture in total income nor the probability that the household head is an agricultural worker register significant changes, and the share of household members in agro-related activities remains flat. This indicates that diversification away from agriculture was not triggered by the protected area expansion.

Broader welfare indicators lead to similar conclusions. Food self-sufficiency months show no significant improvement; if anything, point estimates turn negative towards the later years, though again with large confidence intervals. Similarly, youth schooling rates remain unaffected throughout the observation window .

5.5 (very preliminary) Discussion

At this stage, the first raw estimates point to no robust positive socio-economic impacts on local households. The evidence is more consistent with a neutral or slightly negative effect, particularly in agricultural income, though with considerable statistical uncertainty.
