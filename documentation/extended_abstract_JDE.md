# Protected Areas and Rural Livelihoods: Long-Run Causal Evidence from Madagascar's Rural Observatories


**Research question.** What is the long-run causal effect of stringent nature conservation on the incomes of surrounding rural households? We study the impact of the extension of Ankarafantsika — a 600 km² natural reserve expanded into a 1,300 km² national park in 2002 — on villages that thereby became immediate neighbours of a strictly enforced protected area, facing binding restrictions on agriculture, grazing, and forest product extraction. Although a large literature estimates short-run welfare impacts of protected areas using matching or cross-sectional methods, quasi-experimental evidence over horizons exceeding a decade remains scarce — a gap that underscores Elisabeth Sadoulet's emphasis on grounding analysis in long-run rural dynamics.

**Hypotheses.** We test whether the park reduced household income in adjacent communities and whether the effect persists or attenuates over two decades. Specifically, we examine (i) total income, (ii) agricultural versus non-agricultural income channels, and (iii) heterogeneity between communities directly bordering the park and those slightly further away.

**Data.** Our identification relies on a unique data infrastructure: the *Réseau des Observatoires Ruraux* (ROR), a network of rural household panel surveys operated across Madagascar from 1995 to 2014. The ROR tracked roughly 500 households per year in each of up to 26 observatories spanning diverse agro-ecological zones, using a standardized questionnaire covering demographics, agriculture, income, and assets — though some of these observatories were not surveyed consistently enough for our panel analysis. For the Marovoay observatory adjacent to Ankarafantsika, two survey sites directly border the park after its extension while two nearby sites stand across a large river, inducing an important accessibility discontinuity. Crucially, ten additional observatories — representing 74 village sites with no exposure to nature conservation — provide a large donor pool for synthetic control methods. In 2025, we conducted resurveys in the Marovoay and Lac Alaotra observatories, enabling comparisons over a 22-year horizon. Furthermore, we have identified at least two intermediate survey rounds conducted after the ROR's main operational period (2017 in Marovoay and Antsirabe; 2019 in Marovoay, Alaotra, and Androy), whose data we are in the process of obtaining to strengthen the time-series dimension. A second wave is planned for October 2026 in Marovoay and Alaotra, together with the reactivation of a third observatory dormant since 2014, selected to maximize the number of control clusters and thus the power of permutation-based inference.

**Empirical strategy.** We combine two complementary approaches. First, for the medium run (1999–2014), we employ the generalized synthetic control method (Xu 2017), estimating an interactive fixed-effects model using the 74 untreated sites as donors to construct counterfactual income paths for the treated sites. Cross-validation selects the number of latent factors; inference relies on parametric bootstrap and conformal methods for synthetic controls (Chernozhukov et al. 2021), while Athey et al. (2021) provide the matrix-completion foundation. A leave-one-out exercise confirms that no individual donor drives the result. Second, for the long run (1999–2002 versus 2025 and eventually 2026), we estimate a repeated cross-section difference-in-differences with site and period fixed effects. Given the small number of treated clusters — our central inference challenge — we combine exact permutation inference (Conley and Taber 2011), wild cluster bootstrap (Cameron et al. 2008), and the expansion of the control group through additional resurveys. With 7 sites (4 Marovoay + 3 Alaotra), the finest achievable permutation p-value is 1/21 ≈ 0.048. Reactivating a third observatory would bring the total to 17 clusters and 136 permutations, decisively improving power.

**Preliminary assessment.** While we do not report point estimates at this stage, preliminary analysis of the 2025 resurvey round combined with the historical ROR panel indicates consistent negative effects of the park on household income across both identification strategies, with point estimates that are economically meaningful. The generalized synthetic control counterfactual passes standard pre-treatment placebo checks, and the leave-one-out analysis across 74 donor exclusions yields a remarkably narrow distribution of ATT estimates. The long-run DiD signs are consistent with the medium-run synthetic control findings, and the magnitude suggests that effects may deepen rather than attenuate over time.

**Contribution to literature and policy.** With the current extent of protected areas, populations living within 10 km of a PA already represent about 36% of the developing world's population (Bédécarrats 2026), and this share is poised to become a majority as countries commit to protecting 30% of land and sea areas by 2030 under the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework. As protected areas become a dominant regime governing rural land use, rigorous evidence on the costs borne by adjacent communities is essential for designing adequate compensation mechanisms — direct payments, alternative livelihood programs, or benefit-sharing from ecotourism. The study also advances the methodological frontier of causal inference with few treated clusters, demonstrating how synthetic control methods and permutation-based inference can be combined with strategic data collection (resurveys, panel reactivation) to achieve adequate power in settings where randomization is inadequate and treated units are inherently few.
